A1 What is each series made of? 8

Series A — trend + additive seasonality

  1. Components: a steady upward trend and a regular fixed-period seasonality (12 months), plus noise. No cycle: there are no long, irregular swings on top of the trend.
  2. Additive. A ruler along the peaks and a second along the troughs come out parallel — the vertical distance between the seasonal high and the seasonal low is about the same in the first years as in the last. The level rises; the size of the swing does not.
  3. Real quantity: monthly electricity consumption for a slowly growing town, where each winter adds roughly the same fixed number of heating units regardless of how large the base has become.

Series B — trend + multiplicative seasonality

  1. Components: an upward trend and a very strong fixed-period seasonality (12 months), which dominates the picture; plus noise. No cycle.
  2. Multiplicative. The two rulers fan apart: the early cycles are shallow and the late cycles are roughly twice as tall, so the swing grows in proportion to the level. Equivalently, the peak-to-trough gap is a roughly constant percentage of the local level, not a constant number of units.
  3. Real quantity: monthly airline passengers or retail sales, where the December uplift is "+30% on whatever the business is currently doing" rather than a fixed number of extra customers.

Series C — cycle only

  1. Components: a cycle and noise. No trend (the level in 2006 and in 2025 is the same) and no seasonality (the oscillations are of visibly different lengths and different depths — some troughs are shallow, some plunge far below anything nearby).
  2. Not applicable — there is no seasonality to classify. The oscillation has no fixed period, which is precisely what disqualifies it from being seasonality.
  3. Real quantity: a manufacturing new-orders index or a capacity-utilisation index, which swings with the business cycle every few years but at intervals nobody can put in a diary.

Series D — trend (wandering / stochastic), no seasonality

  1. Components: a trend that changes direction — long, slow, smooth excursions (down to 2014, up to 2022, a dip, then up again) with no repetition at any fixed spacing. Arguably a very long cycle; certainly no seasonality and no fixed-period component at all. High short-run persistence, so noise is small relative to the wander.
  2. Not applicable — no seasonality present.
  3. Real quantity: an exchange rate, a commodity price index, or a stock index — a level that drifts because each move builds on the last, with no calendar rhythm.

Explaining the two disagreements

Series Q — the tests are blind to seasonality. Neither test is a test for periodic structure. ADF asks a single narrow question: is there a unit root, i.e. does a shock persist forever? Q is a bounded oscillation that always comes back — it is aggressively mean-reverting, so ADF rejects the unit root hard and returns p < 0.001. KPSS asks whether the level drifts away from a constant; Q's level is dead flat, so KPSS does not reject and returns its ceiling p = 0.10. Both tests answer their own question correctly, and neither question is "does this series repeat?". The seasonality sails straight through. The instrument that would catch it is the ACF (large positive spikes at multiples of the period) or the periodogram (a sharp line at 1/m) — not a hypothesis test on the mean.

Series R — the tests are tests about the first moment only. Both ADF and KPSS are constructed from the behaviour of the mean/level. Neither one inspects the variance at any point in its derivation. R's level is constant, so ADF finds no unit root and KPSS finds no drift, and both report stationarity. But stationarity requires the second moment to be constant too, and R's spread grows visibly. Growing variance is simply outside the hypothesis being tested. The instruments that catch it are a rolling-standard-deviation plot, a plot of local spread against local level, or the fanning shape itself — and the fix is a variance-stabilising transform before any test is run.

The general lesson, which is the point of the question: ADF and KPSS together are necessary but not sufficient. They jointly pin down the level, and nothing else. Passing both is not a licence to fit ARIMA — you must additionally look at the ACF (for leftover seasonality) and at the spread over time (for non-constant variance). This is the same lesson as B4 and it recurs empirically in C5.2.

  1. Two periodic components, one small peak near the left edge and one tall peak in the middle.

    Period is always the reciprocal of frequency: a component at 0.083 cycles per observation completes one cycle every 1/0.083 ≈ 12 observations. Note the two frequencies are in a 1:10 ratio, so the slow component is exactly ten times the length of the fast one — and because 120 is not an integer multiple that lands on a harmonic of 12 in a way that would make it spurious (harmonics of a 12-period wave sit at 1/6, 1/4, 1/3 …, i.e. to the right of f₂, not to the left), the low-frequency peak is a genuine second component and not an artefact of the first.

  2. Only the 12-observation component is visible in the left-hand panel. The 120-observation component is nearly impossible to see there for three compounding reasons. First, the window shows 240 observations, which is exactly two cycles of a 120-period wave — the eye recognises rhythm by counting repetitions, and two repetitions is not enough to register as a rhythm rather than as "the level happened to drift up and then down". Second, it is far weaker: its peak in the periodogram is roughly a fifth the height of the 12-period peak, so its contribution is a gentle undulation buried under a much larger fast wiggle plus noise. Third, a slow smooth movement is exactly what the eye interprets as trend or wander rather than as periodicity, so even when it is perceived it is mentally filed under the wrong heading. The periodogram has no such difficulty because it is not counting repetitions by eye — it projects the whole series onto every frequency at once and reports the energy, and 1,000-plus observations give the low-frequency bin plenty of resolution.

  3. The 12-month component: yes, that is seasonality. With monthly observations a period of 12 is exactly one calendar year. It is a fixed period, known in advance, and tied to the calendar — you can say today what the phase will be in March 2031. That is the definition of seasonality.

    The 120-month component: no, that is a cycle. 120 months is 10 years. It fails the test on every count: the period is far longer than a year, it corresponds to no calendar unit, and nothing in the calendar recurs on a ten-year basis. Real components at that scale (business cycles, investment cycles, sunspot-like phenomena) have variable length — a "10-year" cycle in practice runs 8 to 13 years, so the sharp line in this periodogram is a property of a clean synthetic series, and in real data the same component would appear as a broad low-frequency band rather than a spike. Lecture terminology, exactly: seasonality has a fixed known period; a cycle oscillates without one.

A5 Judge the remainder 4

Model 1 is finished. Model 2 is not.

Evidence that Model 1 is finished — all four checks pass

  1. Remainder over time: a structureless band of noise around zero, with no visible pattern, no drift, and a spread that stays the same width from observation 0 to 300.
  2. ACF: every spike from lag 1 onwards lies inside the shaded confidence band, with no spike anywhere near the boundary and no pattern in the signs. That is the definition of white noise: nothing left to predict.
  3. Distribution: a single mode, roughly symmetric about zero, tails thinning smoothly on both sides — approximately normal, centred where it should be.
  4. Scale: the remainder is confined to roughly ±2.5, small relative to the series it came from.

Evidence that Model 2 is unfinished — the same four checks, all failing

  1. Remainder over time: a dense, regular, sustained oscillation is plainly visible. The remainder is not noise; it is still swinging, at a constant rate, across the whole span.
  2. ACF — the decisive panel: huge spikes far outside the band, alternating in sign in a fixed repeating pattern, and — critically — not decaying at all out to lag 30. Undamped alternating spikes are the signature of a leftover deterministic periodic component. If it were merely an AR or MA process the spikes would die away.
  3. Distribution: bimodal — two humps with a dip in the middle, rather than one peak at zero. A sinusoid spends most of its time near its turning points and little time crossing zero, so its marginal distribution is U-shaped (the arcsine distribution). Seeing two humps in a residual histogram is near-proof of an unremoved wave.
  4. Scale: the remainder spans roughly ±4, about 60% wider than Model 1's, because it still contains signal that should have been explained.

What structure was left behind, and its period

A seasonal (fixed-period) component that the decomposition failed to remove. Its period is read directly off the ACF: the positive peaks fall at lags 7, 14, 21 and 28 (values ≈ +0.68, +0.65, +0.66, +0.60), with the deepest negative values at lags 3–4, 10–11, 17–18 and 24–25 — the half-period offsets. Spikes at every multiple of 7 with troughs midway between means:

period ≈ 7 observations

If these are daily observations that is a weekly cycle, and the person fitting Model 2 has almost certainly decomposed with the wrong period (or with no seasonal term at all). The fix is to re-run the decomposition with period=7, or to seasonally difference at lag 7, and then re-check the same three panels.

Part BReason it out28 marks

B1 The shuffle test, in your own words 4

A histogram records only how many times each value occurred; the position of each observation on the time axis is discarded the moment the bars are drawn. Shuffling is a permutation, and a permutation changes only the order — it leaves the multiset of values, and therefore every bar height, exactly as it was. So any statistic computed from the histogram is a function of the value counts alone, and must return an identical answer before and after the shuffle, by construction rather than by coincidence: this covers the mean, median, variance, skewness, kurtosis, every quantile, the min and the max. A statistic that can tell them apart is the autocorrelation coefficient — for instance the lag-1 autocorrelation r₁ — because it is computed from pairs (yₜ, yₜ₋₁) of neighbouring observations, so it reads the ordering that the histogram threw away.

B2 Season or cycle? 6

The one-line test I applied throughout: is the period fixed, known in advance, and locked to the calendar or clock? Yes → seasonality. Recurs but with variable, unpredictable length → cycle. Does not recur with any period at all → neither.

B3 Why did differencing not finish the job? 5

What was still in the series: the annual seasonality — the 12-month pattern of summer peaks and winter troughs that is the airline data's most obvious feature. The log fixed the growing amplitude and the first difference removed the trend, and the resulting series does look flat on average; but its expected value still depends on which month it is, so the series is still not stationary and ADF still sees structure. Hence p = 0.071, above 0.05.

Why a first difference cannot remove it. A first difference y′ₜ = yₜ − yₜ₋₁ works on the trend because a locally linear trend contributes the same increment at every step, so consecutive trend terms differ by a constant and the time-dependence cancels. The seasonal term does not have that property. Write the additive model yₜ = Tₜ + Sₜ + εₜ with Sₜ periodic of period 12. Then

y′ₜ = (Tₜ − Tₜ₋₁) + (Sₜ − Sₜ₋₁) + (εₜ − εₜ₋₁)

and the middle bracket is not zero: January's seasonal level and December's seasonal level are different numbers, so they do not cancel. Worse, Sₜ − Sₜ₋₁ is itself periodic with period 12 — differencing a wave gives you another wave of the same period, phase-shifted and scaled, never a flat line. Stated generally: one difference annihilates a polynomial in t of degree one; it does not annihilate a periodic function, and no number of ordinary first differences ever will. Taking a second or third difference only injects noise while leaving the 12-month rhythm intact.

What does remove it: a seasonal difference at lag 12,

y″ₜ = yₜ − yₜ₋₁₂

Lag 12 specifically, and why: 12 is the period of the season for monthly data, and periodicity means Sₜ = Sₜ₋₁₂ exactly. Subtracting the same month one year earlier makes the two seasonal terms identical, so they cancel exactly — you are comparing January with January, not January with December. Any other lag would compare mismatched positions in the cycle and leave a residue; lag 12 is the only lag at which the cancellation is exact. (Lag 24, 36 … would also cancel it, but they discard more data for no benefit.)

The complete recipe is therefore: log (fix the variance) → first difference, d = 1 (remove the trend) → seasonal difference at lag 12, D = 1 (remove the season). That is precisely the specification of the classic airline model, SARIMA(0,1,1)(0,1,1)₁₂.

B4 The test that was wrong 4

ADF tests one thing only — whether there is a unit root in the level — so a p-value below 0.001 tells us the mean is stable and says nothing whatever about the variance, which in this series is visibly non-constant because the swings widen every year. Stationarity requires constant variance as well as constant mean, so please stabilise it first with a log or Box-Cox transform, then re-run ADF alongside KPSS and inspect the ACF and a rolling-standard-deviation plot, and only fit ARIMA once all of those agree.

B5 Order of operations 4

Because the variance-stabilising transform is non-linear and differencing is linear, the two do not commute — and the order that works is log first.

The concrete reason. Take a multiplicative series, yₜ = Tₜ · Sₜ · εₜ, which is what a fanning series is.

Log first, then difference. The log turns the products into sums: log yₜ = log Tₜ + log Sₜ + log εₜ. The noise term is now additive and of constant spread, independent of the level, and the trend has become a plain additive component. One difference then removes the trend cleanly and leaves a residual whose variance is the same in year one as in year ten — exactly what ARIMA assumes.

Difference first, then log. The difference gives TₜSₜεₜ − Tₜ₋₁Sₜ₋₁εₜ₋₁. Every term is still multiplied by the current level, so a 5% wobble is 5 units early in the series and 50 units late in it: the differenced series inherits the growing variance in full, and the problem you were trying to fix is still there. Applying the log afterwards cannot rescue it, for two independent reasons. First, a differenced series contains negative values — roughly half of them — and the logarithm of a negative number is undefined, so the operation is not merely ineffective but impossible. Second, even setting that aside, the log would now be acting on increments rather than on levels, which is not the quantity whose scale was the problem; the multiplicative structure lives in the levels and is destroyed by the time you get there.

Summary: the transform must act on the quantity whose scale grows — the level — and it must act while that quantity is still present. Differencing destroys the level. So: variance first, trend second.

B6 The anomaly a threshold cannot find 5

  1. Which of the three kinds: a contextual (conditional) anomaly. Not a point anomaly, because the value is entirely ordinary in the global distribution; not a collective anomaly, because it is a single observation and no run of points is involved. It is abnormal only given its context — 165 attendances is a normal weekday figure and an impossible Sunday one.

  2. Why no threshold on the raw series can catch it. A threshold on the raw series is, geometrically, a pair of horizontal lines: a rule of the form "flag if y > a or y < b". Such a rule is a function of the value and nothing else. Now look at where 165 sits: weekdays run from 168 to 260, and the Sunday in question is 165, so the anomalous value lies below every ordinary weekday and above every ordinary Sunday — it is in the gap between the two populations, but well inside the overall range.

    • Set the upper line below 165 and you flag the anomaly — and also every single weekday of the year, all of which are 168 or higher. Hundreds of false alarms; the detector is useless.
    • Set the upper line above 260 to spare the weekdays, and 165 passes unnoticed.
    • There is no line in between that does both, because the two requirements are contradictory: you need 165 to be "too high" and 168–260 to be "fine", and 165 < 168.

    The reason is structural, not a matter of choosing the number cleverly: the information that makes 165 anomalous — that it is a Sunday — is not on the y-axis at all. The anomaly lives in the pair (value, day-of-week), and a horizontal line can only see one coordinate of that pair. No amount of tuning gives a one-dimensional rule access to a second dimension. The same argument rules out percentile thresholds, global z-scores and IQR fences, all of which are functions of the value alone.

  3. A two-step procedure that does catch it.

    Step 1 — remove the context, so that "normal for a Sunday" becomes the baseline. Decompose the series with STL at period = 7 and form the remainder, rem = y − trend − seasonal. Each day is now expressed as a deviation from what its own day of the week at that point in the trend should have been. The Sunday's expectation is about 55, so its remainder is roughly +110 while every genuine weekday sits near zero. (Equivalently and more simply: group by day-of-week and compare each Sunday only with other Sundays. STL is the better version because it also tracks the trend.)

    Step 2 — threshold the remainder, robustly. Compute

    z = (rem − median(rem)) / (1.4826 × MAD(rem))

    and flag |z| > 4 or 5. The median and MAD are used rather than the mean and standard deviation because the anomalies themselves would otherwise inflate the scale estimate and hide behind it (masking — demonstrated numerically in C4.4). On the remainder the contextual anomaly is now a plain point anomaly, and a one-dimensional threshold — which failed completely on the raw series — works perfectly, because Step 1 moved the missing dimension onto the y-axis.

    This exact procedure is applied to real data in C4 below, where the equivalent anomaly (187 attendances on Sunday 7 April 2024) scores z = +18.5 and is the single most extreme point in three years of data — while lying comfortably inside the raw series' ordinary range.

The evidence, in three forms:

C2 Find the periods without assuming them 6

The caveat from the lecture: you must de-trend before taking the FFT, not merely subtract the mean. A trend is a very-low-frequency, very-high-energy component; leaving it in dumps a large amount of power into the lowest few frequency bins and, because a finite record of a non-periodic ramp leaks across neighbouring bins, contaminates everything near the left edge — which is exactly where a long-period seasonal component lives. The worksheet's starter code only removes the mean (y = y - y.mean()), and the "and what else?" comment is asking for the de-trending step. Below, y has a fitted straight line removed as well.

Answer: two genuine periods — 7 days and 365 days.

Why the 3.50-day and 2.33-day peaks are not extra rhythms. The weekly pattern is emphatically not a sine wave: it is five high days followed by a cliff and two low days, an asymmetric near-square shape. Fourier analysis represents any non-sinusoidal periodic waveform as a sum of a sine at the fundamental frequency plus sines at every integer multiple of it. So a 7-day non-sinusoidal wave necessarily produces peaks at 7, 7/2 = 3.5, 7/3 = 2.33, 7/4 = 1.75 days and so on, and their presence is evidence about the shape of the weekly cycle, not evidence of additional cycles. The test is arithmetic: divide the fundamental frequency into the candidate and see whether you get an integer. 0.285845 ÷ 0.142466 = 2.006 and 0.428311 ÷ 0.142466 = 3.006 — integers to three significant figures, so both are harmonics. By contrast 0.142466 ÷ 0.002740 = 52.0 — the weekly and annual peaks are related by 52 weeks in a year, which is a coincidence of calendar arithmetic rather than a harmonic relationship, since the annual peak is far weaker and sits at a much lower frequency than the fundamental it would have to be a harmonic of. Reporting "four periods: 7, 3.5, 2.33 and 365 days" would be the classic mistake here.

C3.2 — robust=False: where do the two trends differ most, and why there?

Where: the maximum discrepancy is 5.05 attendances and it occurs on 16–23 July 2024 — immediately before and during the 15-day inflated run of 21 July to 4 August 2024. Averaging the absolute difference in a window around each fault ranks them clearly:

FaultDurationMean |trend difference| nearby
Ramp, 21 Jul – 4 Aug 202415 days4.85

Why there. STL's trend is a LOESS smoother: at each date it fits a local regression over a window of trend=181 days. With robust=False every observation in that window carries full weight, so contaminated points pull the local fit towards themselves in proportion to how far off they are and how many of them there are. With robust=True STL runs additional iterations in which each point receives a bisquare weight computed from the size of its own residual; points whose residuals are large — the anomalies — are down-weighted towards zero, and the trend is refitted as though they were nearly absent. The two curves therefore coincide wherever the data is clean and separate wherever it is not.

The ordering of the three faults is the instructive part, and it is a statement about leverage. The single-day spike moves the trend least (0.95) despite being a 185% excursion, because one contaminated point out of 181 in the smoothing window is diluted to near-irrelevance. The two multi-day faults move it far more, because 12 or 15 consecutive contaminated points sit inside the same window simultaneously and all push the same way — they act like a genuine local shift in level rather than like noise, which is exactly what a trend smoother is built to follow. The ramp does the most damage of all (4.85) because it is both the longest and the most one-sided: every one of its 15 days is biased upward, by an amount that grows to +45%.

Practical conclusion: use robust=True whenever you intend to hunt for anomalies in the remainder. If the trend has already absorbed part of an anomaly, the remainder is correspondingly smaller and the anomaly is harder to detect — the non-robust smoother quietly launders the evidence you are looking for.

C3.3 — the series has two seasonal periods; where did the second one go?

The annual season went into the trend panel.

STL was given a single period, period=7, so its seasonal panel can only contain a 7-day pattern — and it does, and nothing else. The 365-day component did not disappear; it was absorbed almost entirely by the trend component, with only a small residue leaking into the remainder.

The evidence:

  1. The trend panel is not monotone. It rises to 133 in mid-2022, falls to 112 in early 2023, rises to 153 in mid-2023, falls to 125 in early 2024, rises to 166 in mid-2024 and falls again to 123. Peaks and troughs alternate at almost exactly twelve-month intervals. A pure growth trend does not do this.
  2. Isolating it confirms the period exactly. Subtracting the trend's own 365-day moving average — which removes the genuine growth and leaves anything oscillating faster — yields the lower panel above: a clean sinusoid with a period of one year and an amplitude of about 17 attendances, in phase with the mid-year peak.
  3. Only a small residue reaches the remainder. A 31-day moving average of the remainder retains an annual wave of amplitude only ≈ 6.5, against the ≈ 17 sitting in the trend. So roughly three-quarters of the annual signal is in the trend panel and one quarter is spread thinly through the remainder.

Why it lands there. STL's trend smoother has a span of trend=181 days — about half a year, far shorter than the 365-day wavelength of the annual component. A LOESS smoother passes anything that varies slowly relative to its own window and suppresses anything faster. At 365 days the annual wave is comfortably slow enough for a 181-day window to track it, so the smoother follows it faithfully and reports it as "trend". A 7-day wave, by contrast, is far too fast for that window and is pushed out into the seasonal fit, which is what we want. There is nothing wrong with STL here — it is doing precisely what it was configured to do, and the responsibility for specifying both periods lies with the analyst.

How to separate the two properly: use MSTL(s, periods=(7, 365)), which runs STL iteratively and returns a separate seasonal panel per period; or run STL with a trend window much longer than 365 days (e.g. trend=731), which forces the annual wave out of the trend, though it then lands in the remainder rather than in a seasonal panel of its own; or model the annual term explicitly with Fourier regressors. If you only need the weekly pattern and a "trend plus slow movement" summary — which is the common case — the current configuration is perfectly serviceable, provided you do not report the trend panel as though it were pure growth. That last point is exactly the error the colleague makes in Part D.

C4.2 — which of the three kinds is each incident?

  1. Fault 1 — 10 September 2022 — a POINT anomaly. A single observation far from its own expectation, with normal values either side. 97 is not extreme in the global distribution (the series routinely reaches 260), but that is beside the point: it is extreme as a Saturday, and it is isolated. Isolated + extreme-given-expectation = point anomaly.

  2. Fault 2 — 26 August to 6 September 2023 — a COLLECTIVE anomaly (a frozen sensor / stuck reading). This is the clearest case of the three, because no individual value in it is anomalous at all: 161 is an unremarkable weekday count that appears throughout the dataset. What is impossible is the collection — twelve consecutive identical integers, in a series whose day-to-day noise is 5.5%, has probability indistinguishable from zero. The anomaly is a property of the sequence, not of any member of it. Note that only the four weekend days cross |z| > 5 (with z ≈ 14–16), precisely because those are the days on which 161 also violates the weekly context; the eight weekdays hide inside the normal range. A detector that reported only the flagged dates would report "four weekend anomalies in two clusters" and miss the actual mechanism entirely — you have to look at the raw values to see that the sensor is stuck.

  3. Fault 3 — 7 April 2024 — a CONTEXTUAL anomaly. This is B6 realised in data. 187 attendances is thoroughly ordinary in global terms — weekdays span 102 to 261, so 187 sits near the middle of the weekday distribution and no threshold on the raw series could ever isolate it. It is anomalous only given that it is a Sunday, when the other April Sundays are 65, 69 and 72. Once the weekly context is removed by STL it becomes the single most extreme point in three years of data at z = +18.5. The contrast between "invisible to a raw threshold" and "the largest residual in the dataset" is the entire argument for decomposition-based anomaly detection.

  4. Fault 4 — 21 July to 4 August 2024 — a COLLECTIVE anomaly (a gradual drift, sometimes called a level-shift or trend anomaly). The multiplier grows smoothly from 1.00 to about 1.45 across 15 days, so the earliest days are inflated by only a few percent and are entirely unremarkable in isolation — 7 of the 15 days never reach |z| > 5 at all. The event is only visible as a run: a fortnight in which every day is above expectation and the excess keeps growing. Judged one point at a time it would be dismissed as a lucky streak of noise; judged as a sequence it is unmistakable. (This is why a run-based test such as CUSUM, or simply requiring k consecutive same-sign residuals, is a useful complement to a per-point z-score.)

  5. The remaining four incidents — 2022-06-06, 2023-01-17, 2023-04-21/24 and 2024-12-30 — are FALSE ALARMS. Each is one or two isolated days, 30–40 attendances from expectation, which is about 5–6 MAD-sigmas. With 1,095 observations, multiplicative noise of 5.5%, and a residual distribution with slightly heavier tails than the normal, a handful of days beyond 5 sigma over three years is entirely expected. They correspond to no injected fault and to no run.

Why 5. It is the largest threshold at which all four faults are still recovered, and simultaneously the smallest at which the false-alarm rate becomes tolerable. Threshold 3 is unusable for a different reason — 37 incidents over three years is roughly one alert a month with a 72% false-alarm rate, and an alert stream that is wrong three times out of four is one that operators learn to ignore, which converts a detector with good recall into a detector with no effect.

What that choice assumes about the cost of a false alarm. Choosing 5 asserts that a false alarm is moderately expensive but that a missed incident is worse: specifically, that investigating about 5 harmless days spread over three years — under two per year — is an acceptable price for catching every real event. It presumes a human reviews each flag at modest cost, and that the review is cheap enough to absorb a 28% error rate but not cheap enough to absorb 72%. The choice would move if either cost moved. If flags fed an automatic secondary check with no human in the loop, false alarms would be nearly free and I would drop to 3 or 4 to gain sensitivity to the early, gentle days of the ramp. If each flag triggered an expensive manual audit or a public incident report, I would raise it to 6 and accept that isolated single-day point anomalies will be missed — relying on a separate run-based test to catch the multi-day events, which are the ones that survive at 6 anyway. Note also that thresholds trade off unevenly across anomaly types: the collective faults are robust to the choice (Fault 2 holds 4 flagged days at every threshold from 4 to 6), while the point anomaly is entirely at its mercy.

Concretely: at threshold 5 the classical score flags 6 days rather than 18, and the detection rate drops from 4 of 4 faults to 3 of 4. At every threshold from 4 upward the classical detector misses a genuine fault that the robust one catches.

That failure is called masking.

The mechanism. The 27 contaminated days — 12 frozen plus 15 ramp — are themselves included when the mean and standard deviation are computed. Because the standard deviation squares each deviation before averaging, a handful of very large residuals dominate the sum entirely: the anomalies inflate the very yardstick used to measure them, so they end up being measured against a ruler they themselves stretched. Each anomaly hides behind the others, and large ones hide small ones — here the frozen block and the ramp between them roughly double the SD, and that doubling is what pushes the modest 5.88 spike below the threshold. The effect worsens as contamination increases, which is the perverse property: the more anomalies your data contains, the fewer of them a classical z-score will find.

Why median and MAD do not suffer from it. Both have a breakdown point of 50% — you can corrupt just under half the data arbitrarily badly without moving either estimate appreciably, because both depend on the rank of the observations rather than on their magnitudes. Here 27 contaminated days out of 1,095 is 2.5% of the sample, far below that limit, so the robust centre and scale describe the clean majority and the anomalies are measured against a ruler they had no hand in setting. This is the whole reason the lecture specifies median and MAD rather than mean and SD, and the constant 1.4826 is simply the factor that makes the MAD an unbiased estimate of σ for normally distributed data, so the two scores remain comparable in units.

No — I do not believe the tests.

The autocorrelation at lag 7 is +0.961, which is 16 times the width of the significance band and about as close to the theoretical maximum of 1.0 as a real series ever gets. Lags 14, 21 and 28 are 0.948, 0.942 and 0.935 — barely decaying at all. This is not a trace of leftover seasonality; the weekly cycle is completely intact, entirely undamaged by anything done so far, and it remains the single largest feature of the series. Knowing the value seven days ago tells you 92% of the variance of today's value. A series in that condition is manifestly not stationary in any useful sense, and fitting a model to it on the strength of two p-values would be a serious error.

Why both tests were fooled — and it is the same reason as A3's series Q. ADF asks exactly one question, whether shocks persist indefinitely, and a bounded periodic component always returns to where it was, so it contributes no unit root and ADF rejects the null decisively. KPSS asks whether the level wanders from a constant, and a zero-mean periodic component has a perfectly constant long-run level, so KPSS fails to reject. Both tests answer their own question correctly. Neither test has any power against periodic structure, because neither is looking for it.

The lesson, stated once for the whole practical: ADF and KPSS jointly certify the level, and nothing more. They are necessary, never sufficient. The ACF is not an optional extra diagnostic to run if you have time — it is the only instrument in the standard toolkit that can see seasonality, and skipping it is what makes the analysis in Part D fail.

Diagnostic 1 — the variance. Differencing is supposed to reduce variance when it removes a genuine non-stationary component; if there really is a stochastic trend, taking it out leaves a quieter series. Route A's extra first difference does the opposite: it increases the variance by 57%, from 0.0139 to 0.0218. That is the classic sign that the difference removed something which was not there, and injected fresh noise while doing so — a difference of two noisy numbers is noisier than either. The lecture's rule is to stop at the smallest order at which the variance stops falling; here it has already started rising, so we have gone one step too far.

Diagnostic 2 — the lag-1 autocorrelation. Route A's final series has r₁ = −0.417, a large negative value. Differencing an already-stationary series always induces a negative lag-1 correlation — differencing white noise gives a theoretical r₁ of exactly −0.5 — so a strongly negative r₁ is the signature diagnostic for over-differencing, and −0.417 is uncomfortably close to that −0.5 limit. Route B's +0.215 is a modest positive value indicating ordinary short-memory structure that a model should capture with an AR or MA term, not something to difference away.

I would ship Route B: log, then a single seasonal difference at lag 7. Both diagnostics agree, they agree strongly, and Route B is also the simpler pipeline and discards fewer observations (1,088 against 1,087, marginal here but a real consideration on short series).

What that tells you about the trend in this series

The first difference was unnecessary, and that is a substantive finding, not a technicality. It means the series contains no unit root and no stochastic trend — the level is not a random walk in which shocks accumulate permanently. Once the weekly cycle is taken out, the level is already mean-reverting on its own, so d = 0 and only D = 1, m = 7 is needed. Three independent pieces of evidence support this:

  1. The trend is deterministic and saturating, not stochastic. Annual means run 120.5 → 140.0 → 149.3: a gain of 19.5 in the second year but only 9.3 in the third. The growth rate is roughly halving each year — the curve is flattening towards an asymptote, which is bounded behaviour. A random walk is unbounded by construction and never flattens.
  2. The STL trend turns over. It rises by 53 attendances across the first half of the record and falls by 27 across the second. A series with a genuine unit root does not systematically reverse; this reversal is the annual seasonal component sitting inside the trend panel (C3.3) plus the saturation, both of which are bounded deterministic features.
  3. The ADF failure on the log series was a false signal. ADF returned p = 0.152 on log, which looks like evidence of a unit root and is what motivates taking a first difference in the first place. But ADF is known to lose power badly in the presence of strong seasonality and slowly-saturating deterministic trends, both of which are present here — and the moment the weekly cycle is removed by the seasonal difference, ADF drops to essentially zero. The apparent unit root was the weekly cycle and the saturating curve masquerading as one.

Practical upshot for modelling: specify SARIMA(p, 0, q)(P, 1, Q)₇ on the logged series rather than (p, 1, q)(P, 1, Q)₇. If the slow saturating growth or the annual wave still needs handling, the right tool is an explicit deterministic term — Fourier regressors at period 365.25, or a damped-trend exponential smoothing — rather than a second differencing operation, since differencing is the wrong instrument for a bounded deterministic trend and, as the diagnostics above show, actively degrades the series.

Error 2forecast

A significant slope treated as a licence to extrapolate three years, with no interval

What is wrong. Two separate mistakes are compressed into "the fit is statistically significant with a very small p-value, so I am confident attendances will keep rising at that rate".

  • Significance is not predictive validity. The p-value (6.7 × 10⁻⁹) tests one narrow null: that the slope is exactly zero within the observed window. It says nothing about whether that rate continues past the last observation. With n = 1,095, an utterly trivial slope becomes "highly significant"; here it is significant and explains 3% of the variance, which is exactly the situation where significance and usefulness come apart.
  • The extrapolation runs three years past the data, straight through the saturation. Carrying 10.8/year forward gives about 174 by January 2027. The trend has already flattened near 150, so the projection over-states growth by roughly a factor of two on the increment — and, worse, it will keep over-stating by more every year, because the error compounds as the true curve levels off and the line does not. Budgeting three permanent posts on that basis is a real, expensive decision resting on an extrapolation the data does not support.
  • No prediction interval is given at all. A forecast three years out with no uncertainty attached, from a model that explains 3% of the variance, is presented as though it were a measurement.
  • The wrong quantity is forecast for the decision at hand. Staffing is driven by peak load, not by the mean: Mondays average 184 against a Sunday average of 55. A forecast of the average day is close to useless for a rota.

What should have been done. Fit a model that contains the weekly seasonality (SARIMA, or Holt–Winters), validate it with rolling-origin evaluation rather than a single split, benchmark it against seasonal naive, and produce forecasts with prediction intervals over a horizon justified by that validation — a few weeks to a few months for daily clinic data, certainly not three years. Forecast the day-of-week profile, not just the mean. And state plainly that any multi-year projection is a scenario, not a forecast.

Error 4stationarity

"I did not need to transform anything because the mean is what matters"

What is wrong. Stationarity requires a constant variance as well as a constant mean, and this series does not have one. The seasonality is multiplicative: the weekly swing grows in proportion to the level, with corr(level, weekly range) = 0.635, the mean weekly range rising from 111.6 in the lowest-level weeks to 150.5 in the highest (C1.3). The colleague's own second figure is the proof — the differenced series visibly fans out, its band noticeably wider through 2024 than through 2022. ADF and KPSS are both constructed entirely from the behaviour of the level and neither inspects the second moment at any point, so passing them says nothing about the variance; this is precisely the failure of series R in A3 and the error corrected in B4. Feeding a heteroskedastic series to ARIMA violates the model's constant-variance assumption, which makes the prediction intervals wrong — too wide early in the record and too narrow late in it, which is the dangerous direction.

What should have been done. Take logs (or a Box–Cox transform) first, which converts the multiplicative structure to additive and stabilises the variance in a single step, and only then difference — variance first, trend second, for the reasons set out in B5 (the two operations do not commute, and after differencing the series contains negative values on which the log is undefined). Verify with a rolling-standard-deviation plot rather than assuming it worked.

Error 5outliers

A global mean ± 3 SD threshold on the raw series, and "no flags" read as "clean data"

What is wrong. This is the most serious error of the five, because it produced a confident all-clear on data containing four genuine faults. Work through the arithmetic: the series has mean 136.65 and standard deviation 53.77, so the band is

136.65 ± 3 × 53.77 = [ −24.6 , 298.0 ]

while the data ranges from 37 to 261. The band is wider than the data on both sides, so it was arithmetically impossible for any day to be flagged. "Not a single day was flagged" is not a finding about the data; it is a restatement of how the threshold was constructed.

Three compounding faults:

  • The standard deviation is measuring the seasonality, not the noise. The SD of 53.77 is dominated by the weekly cycle — Monday 184 against Sunday 55 — not by the day-to-day variability. Using it as a yardstick for "unusual" makes the yardstick roughly eight times too long: the robust scale of the STL remainder is 6.66. Any global-SD threshold on a strongly seasonal series inherits this problem.
  • A raw threshold is structurally incapable of finding three of the four faults, however the number is chosen (B6). The contextual Sunday of 7 April 2024 is 187 attendances — squarely inside the ordinary weekday range of 102 to 261 — so no pair of horizontal lines can isolate it without flagging most of the year. The 12-day frozen block reads exactly 161 every day, a perfectly ordinary weekday value. The 15-day ramp peaks at 261, which is the series maximum and therefore inside any band that does not flag it as a single point.
  • "No flags, therefore clean" inverts the correct inference. Finding zero anomalies in 1,095 days of real operational data should prompt the question "is my detector working?", not the conclusion "the data is perfect". A detector that cannot fire is indistinguishable from a detector that fires correctly and finds nothing — unless you check its band against the data range, which takes one line.

What should have been done. Threshold the remainder of a decomposition, not the raw series, using a robust scale:

res = STL(s, period=7, robust=True, seasonal=15, trend=181).fit()
rem = s - res.seasonal - res.trend
z   = (rem - rem.median()) / (1.4826 * np.median(np.abs(rem - rem.median())))
flagged = s.index[np.abs(z) > 5]

This finds all four faults — the 10 September 2022 spike (z = 5.9), the 26 August – 6 September 2023 frozen block (z up to 16.3), the 7 April 2024 contextual Sunday (z = 18.5) and the 21 July – 4 August 2024 ramp (z up to 13.5) — at a cost of five false-alarm days over three years. The median and MAD must be used rather than the mean and SD, because the anomalies would otherwise inflate the scale estimate and conceal themselves; with the classical estimator the September 2022 spike is missed outright and the ramp collapses from seven flagged days to one (C4.4). Finally, the multi-day faults should be caught by a run-based rule as well — several consecutive same-sign residuals, or a check for repeated identical values, which would catch the frozen sensor instantly and independently.
